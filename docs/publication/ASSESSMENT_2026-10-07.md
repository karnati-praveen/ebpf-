# Shardwise: publication assessment

Assessment date: 7 October 2026. Target: a reputable IEEE/ACM specialist conference. This is a time-bounded research review with two independent subagents, one examining novelty and one examining technical rigor. It is an editorial judgment, not a calibrated prediction of a program committee decision.

**My current estimate is about 20%, with a reasonable subjective range of 15–25%, for one submission to a well-matched specialist full-paper track.** The manuscript contains a plausible publishable empirical study, but its present evidence and differentiation leave it vulnerable to rejection. A local application demo is useful; it does not establish the missing comparative performance results. Public repository/demo access is planned or unavailable for this review, based on your latest clarification.

Workshop and demo acceptance are different publication categories from main-conference full-paper acceptance. These are estimates for one appropriately selected submission, not cumulative probabilities over repeated submissions.

**What the manuscript already does well.** The problem is practical: distributing a small model can add overhead, and a short session may end before a migration repays its cost. The research questions connect architecture, concurrency, adaptation, and measurement. The paper reports real-model execution: 65 completed runs and 939 requests, rather than only simulations. Those counts are manuscript claims; I did not recompute them from raw logs.

The most persuasive results are the uncomfortable ones: a 25% fault-phase gain becomes roughly 5–7% over the full run, adapting under delay loses roughly 6–8%, and the remaining-work rule does not solve continuously renewed demand. Retaining failures, including drain time, distinguishing synthetic diagnostics from real-model evidence, and treating runs as the analysis unit are good methodological decisions. The paper also candidly limits generalization. Its writing and figures are substantially more mature than an early project proposal.

The ordered-partition recurrence and the fixed-horizon payback algebra appear correct under the stated cost model. Their correctness is different from originality or accuracy on a real runtime. The paper itself acknowledges that the constituent principles are established. Keeping that candor will help reviewers trust the narrower empirical contribution.

**What is actually new, and what overlaps prior work.** The defensible contribution is a compact CPU runtime and an empirical examination of objective choice, disruptive reconfiguration, biased transition forecasts, and misleading telemetry in a small deployment. The evidence does not support presenting layer splitting, sum versus bottleneck objectives, replay recovery, or amortized migration as new general mechanisms.

| [DiSCo](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11417v1), §4.3 | Gates generation migration using remaining decode savings versus overhead; avoids transferring KV state and sends token IDs. | Payback and token-based handover are prior principles. DynoPipe was inspected by the novelty subagent from the author-hosted PDF; the parent's separate web fetch failed. That is a retrieval limitation, not evidence that the reference is invalid. Its heterogeneous GPU edge/cloud deployment should be described accurately. Table 1's phrase “datacenter-class speed” is less precise than naming its hardware and measured transition mechanism.

A further recent source, [Pre-Compiled Pipeline Shards for Distributed LLM Inference on Intel AI PC Fleets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19147), is an August 2026 preprint about commodity-device pipeline inference and multiuser interleaving. It is useful context, not verified peer-reviewed acceptance evidence. It does not establish the same adaptive-recovery contribution.

**The largest experimental weakness is the baseline.** Section 5.1 uses one thread per worker. At concurrency two, latency placement keeps the model on one machine, while throughput placement uses workers on both machines. The 80.5% gain is therefore an informative comparison inside your runtime, but it also compares different amounts of active compute. It does not establish superiority over a resource-matched serving alternative.

Because Qwen3-0.6B fits on either 8 GiB VM, a reviewer can reasonably ask: why not put a complete model on each machine and route each request to the first available replica? Section 7.2 already acknowledges synthetic evidence that a suitable replica dispatcher can match the pipeline. That makes a real-model replica comparison central, rather than optional future work. Also compare an unsplit model using a justified CPU thread configuration. An optimized engine is useful as a practical deployment baseline, with precision and resource differences explicitly controlled or reported. I am not assuming that replicas or an optimized engine will win; the present paper leaves that question unanswered.

**The headline payback mechanism has no demonstrated advantage over the simpler policy.** Table 4 reports whole-run CPU throughput of 6.041 TPS for gate versus 6.133 for hysteresis; under delay, 6.055 versus 6.131; under loss, 5.409 versus 5.443. With only two or three repetitions, these small differences should not be declared significant. The important observation is that the results do not demonstrate a gate advantage. Table 3 likewise has the remaining-work policy below the fixed-horizon policy at concurrency two, and both below starting with the throughput placement.

This can support a useful study of why reasonable controllers fail. It is weaker evidence for a new controller claimed to improve serving. Choose the paper's central claim accordingly: explain and reproduce failure modes, or implement and evaluate a remedy. Do not make the policy look successful by reporting only the fault phase.

**Breadth and uncertainty are insufficient for broad deployment claims.** The evidence is one 0.6B FP32 CPU model, two identically configured Azure VMs, concurrency one or two, short fixed prompts, and injected rather than naturally heterogeneous conditions. Sixty-five total runs do not change the fact that each comparison has only two or three independent repetitions. Additional runs should target the central comparisons, vary run order, and report variation and uncertainty at the run level. Ten repetitions is a sensible starting design here, not a universal rule or a guarantee of adequate power.

The 99.2% queueing result is specifically synthetic loopback evidence. It should not be presented as a measured property of Azure links or home networks. To make it a stronger systems contribution, show the same diagnostic with the real model and actual transport, and measure whether correcting telemetry changes placement decisions and end-to-end results.

**Technical points to clarify before review.** Equation (3) is an ideal capacity upper bound. The transition from sum to bottleneck dominance depends on P/B. The automatic rule “Q greater than one means throughput” is reasonable for the evaluated two-stage case, but is not justified as a general rule for arbitrary worker counts. Larger deployments need a concurrency-aware decision model or evidence showing when that heuristic is adequate.

Equations (5)–(7) are algebraically valid for the chosen fluid surrogate. The finite-cohort interpretation needs care: when the cohort contains only R remaining tokens, real completed work cannot exceed R, and concurrency changes as requests finish. At zero margin, a simple constant-cost makespan condition is T + R*j_n < R*j_c. With margins, explain exactly which quantity receives the margin. Avoid presenting the surrogate as a necessary-and-sufficient guarantee for real finite-session completion time.

A small example illustrates the concurrency qualification without disproving the correctly stated capacity bound. For nine uniform-cost layers on three equal-speed workers, with nonempty remote-worker link costs of one unit each, a three-stage split can minimize B at 4 while P is 11. At Q=2 its envelope cost is max(4,11/2)=5.5. A two-active-stage split has B=5 and P=10, giving max(5,10/2)=5. Thus minimizing B alone can choose a worse finite-concurrency envelope once there are more than two stages. This is a model counterexample, not a measurement of your implementation.

Equation (4), T = F + C*p*L, should explain how multiple active request histories, their context lengths, serialization, and weight-loading work enter the forecast. The missing dependence on active requests is a question to resolve from code and logs, not a confirmed implementation bug. Explicit transition IDs would make the transition-duration analysis more reliable than grouping affected request disruptions.

The greedy-output check covers the 12 demand-matrix runs. It does not, as written, establish exact replay correctness through arbitrary mid-request migrations or failures. Add matched greedy-output checks at several fault/migration positions, confirm that streamed tokens are neither duplicated nor omitted, and state what failures are covered. Recovery after remote-worker loss also does not establish fault tolerance of the router/controller machine or survival when the full model cannot fit on a remaining worker.

The worker-loss comparison establishes that implemented recovery helps relative to a static policy with no recovery. It does not establish better availability than replicated serving, a restart/retry strategy, or another fault-aware runtime. Report interruption duration and user-visible stalls as well as the zero-versus-19 failure count.

**Two numerical wording corrections are concrete.** From Table 2, 4.001/2.419 - 1 = 65.4%, so latency placement has 65.4% higher throughput relative to throughput placement. Reversing the denominator gives a 39.5% throughput loss from choosing the wrong placement. The conclusion's “65.4% loss” should therefore be corrected. Completion time falls from 12.531 to 8.012 seconds, a 36.1% reduction; the abstract's “65.4% faster” should explicitly refer to throughput.

Measured transitions of 5,382 ms versus a 3,054 ms forecast are 76.2% above forecast, but the forecast is 43.3% below the measurement. The abstract's “exceeded forecast” formulation is good. Use it consistently when discussing underestimation so denominators remain clear.

**How the application demo affects the assessment.** A demonstration can show that a second machine joins, layer placement changes, requests overlap, and generation continues after a worker is removed. A live timeline displaying decisions, forecast and measured disruption, active requests, and resumed token generation would support your paper directly. A chat interface alone establishes usability and execution, not comparative efficiency or algorithmic novelty.

For full-paper review, summarize the implementation and demonstration in the paper and provide appropriate artifact access. An unseen demo outside the submission cannot influence reviewers. For a demo track, the application can become the central contribution, with a shorter paper explaining target users, distinguishing capabilities, installation, licensing, and evaluation. The [ACL 2026 demo call](https://2026.aclweb.org/calls/system_demonstration/) is a concrete example requiring both video and a usable system link; it is a past-cycle policy example, not an open submission opportunity. NLP-demo fit would still depend on the program's scope.

**The revisions most likely to change a decision are experimental.** First, measure resource-matched single-machine and two-replica baselines. Second, repeat the central objective and policy comparisons with enough independent runs to characterize variation. Third, test additional context/output lengths, concurrency four and eight, and at least one genuinely heterogeneous or additional-worker configuration. Fourth, isolate the causes of underforecast transition cost and contaminated telemetry with real-model ablations. Fifth, compare the current controller against a corrected or uncertainty-aware controller if you choose a methods contribution. These are recommendations; none was executed in this review.

An empirical-study framing is viable even if replication wins or the gate continues to lose, provided the paper explains the boundary conditions and produces generalizable evidence. More repetitions alone will not resolve weak novelty or a missing baseline. Conversely, adding a new algorithm is not mandatory if the measurement insight becomes strong enough for the selected track.

**My likely review recommendation today is weak reject for a specialist full-paper track, with encouragement to revise.** The reasons would be limited demonstrated differentiation, missing resource-matched alternatives, and insufficient evidence for generalization or the payback mechanism's benefit. A focused workshop or an application-oriented demo paper is a more plausible near-term route; a strengthened empirical paper remains a credible longer route.
